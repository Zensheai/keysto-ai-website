? How it works

The simple math behind your rate

No mystery here. This is the same back-of-the-napkin math a smart accountant would walk you through — we just do it instantly.

  1. Add up what you need.

    Your monthly take-home goal plus your business expenses is the money the business has to actually bring in.

  2. Add a buffer.

    We pad that number with your slow-month cushion, so one quiet month doesn't wreck your year.

  3. Count only the hours you get paid for.

    You don't bill 40 hours a week — admin, sales, and marketing eat into it. We price against the hours clients actually pay for.

  4. Divide.

    Revenue needed ÷ paid hours = your hourly rate. The recommended rate includes your buffer; the minimum rate doesn't.

? How it works

How the score becomes a verdict

Seven criteria, each worth up to 5 points, for a total out of 35. We turn that into a percentage and a plain-English call.

  1. Strong yes — 74% and up.

    The money, the fit, and your gut all line up. This is the kind of work worth saying yes to.

  2. Proceed with caution — 50% to 73%.

    There's something here, but a weak spot or two. Fix or negotiate them before you commit.

  3. Pass on this one — under 50%.

    Too many warning signs. Saying no frees you up for work that's a better fit.

  4. Watch your weakest score.

    Even on a "yes," your lowest-rated answer is the thing most likely to bite you. We call it out so you can plan for it.

? How it works

What goes into the estimate

A simplified version of how a solo business owner's taxes are figured. Real returns have more moving parts — this gets you in the ballpark.

  1. Self-employment tax first.

    When you work for yourself you cover both halves of Social Security and Medicare — about 15.3% on most of your net income.

  2. Then federal income tax.

    We subtract the standard deduction and half your self-employment tax, then apply the federal brackets to what's left.

  3. Add your state.

    Each state is different. We apply a simplified estimated rate — and nine states have no income tax at all.

  4. Split it into four.

    The IRS expects estimated taxes quarterly. We divide your total across the four due dates so nothing piles up.
